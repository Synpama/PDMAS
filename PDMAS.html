PDMAS: Profiling for Distributing Multi-Agent System

1. Analysis of the model composition

1.1: Agents and Roles in a Distributed Context

Clearly define the criteria for the distribution of the model:

  • What types of agents make up the system, and how are their roles and responsibilities defined or distinguished?

  • Do some agents play a central role, in coordination or in collecting/distributing information? What is the nature of their knowledge of the system (local vs global)?

  • Are agents’ roles and responsibilities fixed or dynamic? How do these changes affect system management, particularly in a distributed context?

  • Are agents located in an explicit space, and does this location influence their behavior or interactions?

  • Is there a hierarchy between agents or resilience mechanisms in case of failure of a critical agent?

  • Questions:

    • Typology of agents and their responsibilities

      • Does the system contain different types of agents?

        • Yes, clearly identified by role/function
        • Yes, but roles are fuzzy or overlapping
        • No, all agents are similar

      • Can agents be grouped into functional families?

        • Yes, several distinct groups (e.g., producers, consumers)
        • No, each agent is unique in its function
        • Partially, some agents share common tasks

      • Does each agent have a fixed role in the system?

        • Yes, static and well-defined role
        • No, roles evolve during execution
        • Some roles are fixed, others are dynamic

      • Do some agents have critical responsibilities (e.g., global impact)?

        • Yes, multiple agents with critical roles
        • Yes, a single critical agent
        • No, responsibilities evenly distributed

      • Is there a hierarchy among agents?

        • Yes, clearly defined superior and subordinate agents
        • No, all agents are on equal footing
        • Flexible or temporary hierarchy

    • Central-role agents and global view

      • Are there agents with a global vision of the system?

        • Yes, one or more agents have global knowledge
        • No, no agent has a global vision

      • Do some agents coordinate the actions of other agents?

        • Yes, centralized coordination by one or more agents
        • No, distributed coordination
        • No, coordination absent

      • Do some agents collect or distribute information to most others?

        • Yes, “hub” agents or information relays
        • No, peer-to-peer exchanges without centralization

      • What happens if a central agent is isolated? The level of dependence on the central agent?

        • The system no longer functions properly
        • There are fallback or redundancy mechanisms
        • The impact is limited, not critical

      • Is the central role fixed or dynamic?

        • Fixed, assigned at system initiation
        • Dynamic, evolves based on context (e.g., election)

    • Agent mobility and localization

      • Are agents located in an explicit space?

        • Yes, defined physical or topological space
        • No, abstract agents without location

      • Can agents move?

        • Yes, with clear rules and defined frequency
        • No, fixed agents
        • Limited or conditional mobility

      • Does an agent's behavior depend on its position?

        • Yes, important local interactions
        • No, behaviors independent of location

      • Is the space represented by:

        • A continuous space (e.g., real coordinates)
        • A grid or discrete mesh
        • A graph or network (nodes and edges)

      • Do agents interact more frequently with agents close in space?

        • Yes, strong spatial correlation
        • No, interactions independent of location

        • It depends on the type of message or context

      • Are some exchanges critical for system consistency?

        • Yes, several interactions essential to proper functioning
        • No, the system tolerates losses/desynchronizations
        • Partially, depends on the type of interaction

    • Interaction structure

      • Do some agents interact significantly more than others?

        • Yes, "core agents" or communication hubs

      • What interaction mode is primarily used by the agents?

        • Message passing between agents
        • Observation of the environment or other agents

2. Technical challenges of distribution

2.1 Distributed Couplings and Dependencies

A component refers to an autonomous functional unit of the MAS that may correspond to an agent, a group of agents, or a functional module...

  • To what extent do the distributed components of the MAS depend on each other to ensure correct operation?

  • Are there tightly coupled components requiring frequent synchronization that could impact performance in a distributed environment?

  • How could network latency and message loss between components affect the consistency and validity of model results?

  • Questions:

    • Dependencies Between Components

      • Does the proper functioning of one component strongly depend on another?

        • Low – components almost independent, limited exchanges, autonomous operation
        • Partial – components interact but can operate partially independently; can progress for some time without the other
        • High – correct operation strongly depends on frequent exchanges or responses from the other

      • Do the components need to exchange information at every simulation step?

        • Yes, at every or almost every step (tight synchronization)
        • No, only at certain events/conditions
        • No, or rarely — sporadic or delegated updates

      • Do exchanges between components involve a significant or frequent volume of data?

        • Yes, transfers are heavy and frequent
        • No, light or infrequent exchanges
        • Intermediate: moderate, depends on the scenario

      • Do the components share a common state that must remain consistent?

        • Yes, immediate consistency required
        • Yes, some desynchronization is acceptable
        • No, independent states

      • Do components require frequent access to shared data or states?

        • Yes, very frequent access
        • Occasionally
        • No, rare or no access

    • Synchronization between components

      • Does the system contain strongly coupled components requiring frequent synchronization?

        • Yes — fine synchronization required (strong consistency)
        • Partially — moderate synchronization sufficient
        • No — weak coupling, asynchronous acceptable

      • Must component A wait for a response from component B to continue execution?

        • Yes, there is an explicit wait (blocking call)
        • No, A sends the information and continues (asynchronous)
        • Sometimes — depends on the context or scenario

  • Are distributed locking mechanisms or other concurrency control techniques required? What would be their impact on the model’s performance and complexity?

        • Objects manipulated or modified by multiple agents

      • Can simultaneous access to these resources cause conflicts or undesired effects?

        • Yes, conflicts are possible without regulation mechanisms
        • No, access is naturally desynchronized or independent
        • Partially, it depends on the system’s conditions or state

    • Locking and access control

      • Does the model include mechanisms to manage concurrent access to these resources?

        • Yes, through explicit locks or exclusive access
        • Yes, using a queue, priorities or time-based coordination
        • No, conflicts are not specifically managed

      • How frequently are concurrent accesses likely to occur?

        • Very frequently (at nearly every simulation step)
        • Occasionally (depending on situations or specific interactions)
        • Rarely or in exceptional cases

      • What is the potential impact of uncoordinated access to a resource?

        • Critical effects or major inconsistencies in the model
        • Abnormal but non-blocking behavior
        • No notable impact

2.3 Consistency and Synchronization

  • What level of data consistency is required between distributed components? Is strong (immediate) consistency necessary, or is eventual consistency acceptable?

  • Are precise temporal synchronization mechanisms required between distributed components? What are the tolerances for desynchronization?

  • How is global system consistency maintained in the presence of delays or potential failures of distributed components?

  • Questions:

    • Consistency of shared data

      • What level of consistency is required between components for the model to remain valid?

        • Strong consistency: each component must immediately have an accurate view of others' data
        • Causal consistency: events must be perceived in a logical order (e.g. cause before effect)
        • Eventual consistency: a slight delay in data updates is acceptable

      • Do some shared data need to remain accurate at all times, even in the short term?

        • Yes, for several critical variables
        • Only for some sensitive states
        • No, delays or errors are acceptable

    • Temporal synchronization

      • Does the model rely on simultaneous (or near simultaneous) evolution of all agents?

        • Yes, agents must progress simultaneously (real-time simulation, strict synchronization)
        • Not necessarily, but a logical order must be preserved
        • No, agents can evolve independently without time constraints

      • Must events be coordinated at the global scale (e.g., all agents change phase simultaneously)?

        • Yes, global synchronization necessary
        • Sometimes, for key steps
        • No, local coordination suffices

      • Is the timing of actions important?

        • Yes, exact timing is crucial
        • An approximate order suffices
        • No, only the final state matters

      • To what extent can the model tolerate some agents being ahead or behind others?

        • Very little: slight lag compromises model validity
        • Moderately: limited lag acceptable if global order is maintained
        • Easily: agents can be desynchronized

    • Causality management in interactions
